The problem

Amplifying microwaves with a beam of electrons

Radars, satellite downlinks and electronic-warfare systems all need to turn a faint microwave signal into a very strong one. Past a few hundred watts, and up into the tens of gigahertz, that job still belongs to a vacuum device: the traveling-wave tube.

Inside it, an electron gun fires a continuous, pencil-thin beam down the axis of the tube. The signal to be amplified is wound around that beam as a helix of wire, which slows its forward march down to the speed of the electrons themselves. Wave and beam then travel together for the length of the tube — and the beam hands its energy over to the wave.

Step 1

Slow the wave down

In free space the wave outruns the electrons several times over. Coiling the wire forces it to take the long way round, dropping its forward speed to a fraction of c — the speed of the beam.

Step 2

Bunch the electrons

The axial field of the wave holds some electrons back and pushes others forward. Within a few centimetres the smooth stream has become a train of dense bunches.

Step 3

Harvest the energy

The beam is set slightly faster than the wave, so the bunches drift into the decelerating phase and give up energy. The signal grows exponentially — 40 to 50 dB end to end.

And all of it rests on the gun

That exchange only works if the beam stays a thin, well-behaved pencil over the whole length of the tube. Space charge pushes it apart, magnetic focusing squeezes it back, and the balance between the two is decided at the source — by the cathode voltage, the shape of the focusing electrode, the anode aperture, the field of the magnets. Get it wrong and the beam scallops outward until it intercepts the helix: the intercepted current becomes heat, and the tube is scrap.

Knowing where the beam actually goes means solving the trajectories — a Monte-Carlo particle-tracking run in ANSYS, hours of CPU for a single configuration. That is affordable when you want to check one design. It is not affordable when you want to explore a design space.

Hence this project: run the expensive simulation a few hundred times, then let a model learn the mapping from gun parameters to beam trajectory. A surrogate that answers in milliseconds turns a verification tool into something you can put inside an optimisation loop.

Approach

From a physics solver to a learned model

No historical dataset existed — nobody had logged thousands of gun designs. The training data had to be manufactured, which made the design of experiments as much a part of the work as the modelling itself.

  1. 01

    Generate the data

    Monte-Carlo sampling of the gun's parameter space, with the resulting beam trajectories computed by the physics solver (ANSYS). Persalys drove the sampling plans and the uncertainty & sensitivity analysis, which also showed which parameters the trajectory is actually sensitive to.

  2. 02

    Find out what the data supports

    Rather than guessing an architecture, H2O AutoML was used to benchmark whole families of models — linear models, random forests, gradient boosting, stacked ensembles — on the same targets. That set the baseline any custom model had to beat.

  3. 03

    Model the trajectory

    A neural network in TensorFlow mapping the gun parameter vector to the beam trajectory, with the feature preparation, scaling and validation split built around the structure of the simulated data.

  4. 04

    Make it usable

    The trained surrogate replaces the solver at inference time: the same question that took hours of CPU is answered in milliseconds, cheaply enough to sweep the parameter space instead of sampling it.
